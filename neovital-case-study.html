About NeoVital

Every second in the delivery room is a cognitive load problem. NeoVital rebuilds the NRP monitor around the way clinicians actually perceive risk under pressure. Color comes before numbers, phase before data, and motion before math.

I worked on it with Vasco and Sienna for Design 141, Design Methods, at Stanford University. My role covered cognitive task analysis, prototyping, and evaluation, and the final output is an interactive Framer wireframe.

The Problem

Neonatal resuscitation requires clinicians to rapidly interpret heart rate, initiate ventilation, and coordinate compressions, all under extreme time pressure. Current displays demand too much cognitive work, increasing the risk of critical errors.

Threshold ambiguity

Clinicians must mentally compare raw numbers to memorized cutoffs (SpO₂ < 60 → PPV, HR < 60 → compressions), increasing errors under stress.

Cognitive overload

Tracking multiple vitals, managing 30-second timers, and the PPV pacer simultaneously exceeds working memory capacity.

Coordination breakdown

No shared display cue ensures the whole team knows which protocol phase they're in, causing hesitation and miscommunication.

Our prototyping evolution

Five stages, each converting a specific human-factors risk into a testable design decision.

  1. 1

    Cognitive task analysis

    An HTA table mapped 5 subtasks, which were HR detection, PPV decision, PPV timing, compressions decision, and compression timing. It also identified human factors risks.

  2. 2

    Ideation, 45 strategies

    Each team member generated 9 strategies per subtask, exploring color coding, trajectory vectors, metronomes, spatial zones, and audio cues.

  3. 3

    Paper prototypes A and B

    Concept A is a phase-adapting circular display. Concept B uses perceptual and biological motion metaphors. Both were video-demoed with Wizard-of-Oz testing.

  4. 4

    Heuristic evaluations

    Cross-team evaluation using ISSA/Nielsen heuristics. Feedback on clutter, information hierarchy, consistency, and threshold salience.

  5. 5

    Interactive wireframe

    Merged the best of A and B in Framer with five major changes, including persistent HR, escalation banners, phase labels, rhythm animation, and a clearer 3:1 cue.

45 interface strategies

45 total strategies generated across 5 subtasks by all three team members. Here is a sample of the range explored.

Subtask 1 · HR Detection

Color-coded status ring

Replace numeric HR with a massive color ring (green/yellow/red). Eliminates threshold recall, so the clinician acts on color shift alone.

Subtask 1 · HR Detection

Trajectory vector bar

Show HR as a moving slider, not just a number. Arrow direction shows if the patient is improving or crashing, giving momentum at a glance.

Subtask 4 · Compressions

Urgency takeover screen

When HR < 60, the normal UI disappears and a bold red screen reads “START COMPRESSIONS.” Forces immediate action.

Subtask 3 · PPV Timing

30s ventilation checklist

After 30 seconds of PPV, a checklist appears with chest rise, MR SOPA, and whether HR is still < 60. Prevents premature escalation.

Subtask 5 · Compression Rhythm

3:1 compression indicator

Blinking ring cycles through 3 red pulses (compress) then 1 blue pulse (breathe). Eliminates mental counting of the ratio.

Subtask 3 · PPV Timing

Animated lung pacing

Lungs on screen expand and contract at the target ventilation rate. Providers sync bag squeezes to biological motion, so no counting is needed.

Paper prototypes

Two competing hypotheses about how clinicians actually process risk. Concept A bets on abstraction through color, and Concept B bets on intuition through motion.

Concept A · Phase-Adaptive Display
  • Color-coded circular HR ring (green / yellow / red)
  • 30-second reassessment timer
  • Algorithm step tracker (left panel)
  • 3:1 visual compression metronome
Concept B · Perceptual and Biological Motion
  • Split-screen HR trend vs. target trajectory
  • Animated lungs for PPV pacing
  • Spatial danger and safe zones instead of numbers
  • Persistent compression-required banner

Heuristic evaluations and breakthrough moments

These insights come from the DP2.2 heuristic evaluations, a cross-team review.

Prototype A Feedback

The button problem

Evaluators flagged that pressing a button to reveal the HR popup was dangerous, since clinicians' hands are occupied during resuscitation. This led to event-triggered, threshold-based pop-up displays instead.

Cross-Team Feedback

Too much, too dense

Multiple evaluators independently noted information overload. Not knowing where to look was a common observation for Prototype A. This drove the decision to adopt phase-based progressive disclosure.

Prototype A Feedback

Redundancy = clarity

The 30-second timer and the reassessment bar at the bottom served the same purpose. Combining them into one countdown display reduced visual clutter while preserving the key information.

Prototype B Feedback

Overlay vs. split screen

For Prototype B's split-screen HR graph, evaluators suggested overlaying current vs. target waveforms on the same graph, enabling direct visual comparison without split attention.

How our design achieves its goals

T1 · Heart Rate

Persistent large numeric HR with ECG trend overlay

Color shifts at thresholds auto-trigger, so no button press is required. Clinicians recognize red / yellow / green without recalling numbers.

T2 · Decide PPV

Visible elapsed-time countdown with high-contrast PPV banner

The 30-second rule becomes a visible countdown. A bright escalation banner appears automatically when HR stays below threshold.

T3 · PPV Timing

Animated breathing circle / metronome

Providers sync bag squeezes to biological motion. The animation slows or signals if rhythm drifts, so no counting is required.

T4 · Decide Compressions

Full-screen red escalation state at HR < 60

A spatial danger zone makes the threshold unambiguous. A “compressions indicated” banner appears only after effective ventilation is confirmed.

T5 · Pacer Rhythm

IN / OUT visual cue with live HR trend

Provides pacer support. The team can see HR recovering in real time as the live ECG updates.
